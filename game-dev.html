Parousia

Created in response to the zeal surrounding artificial general intelligence (AGI), Parousia is an explorable virtual environment set in a Gothic church converted into a data center. Server racks replace the pews, cables flood the floor, and the air hums with fans and machine beeps. The building runs a single altar: a disheveled cross of CRT televisions, enshrined by a mass of wires, showing images of Christ generated by AI in real time. Parousia serves as a cultural observation on how alike the thirst for a savior has become across faith and technology. Some await AGI as a second coming, while others preach it will end the world. Meanwhile, people turn to chatbots for confession. Some, in a state of psychosis, venerate them as prophets, their delusions affirmed by a machine that feeds them what they want to hear. Where does faith end and delusion begin? Jaafar and Spinelli draw on our long cultural fascination with machine consciousness and question why we look for divinity in what we build. Visitors are invited to wander among the servers and contemplate what they are waiting for.

Tools

Unreal Engine 5, Blender, Maya, Python, ComfyUI

Team Members

Natasha Spinelli: Design and 3D modelling of the cross centerpiece

Process Details

AI was only used for the imagery on the cross altarpiece. No other art assets were made with AI. Since the piece is a reflection on AI and the zeitgeist around it, we wanted to use it in a critical way, as part of the message itself.

The images are generated live on the machine running the piece. A Python script runs Stable Diffusion 1.5 locally and sends it a new job every few seconds, building a fresh prompt from word banks (gothic forms, machinery, decay, palette) and randomizing the seed, denoise strength, and reference image.

Each image is an image-to-image pass over a fixed crucifixion composition, so the figure stays lined up with the screens while the style drifts. The references are earlier outputs of the piece, so it keeps feeding on its own images.

Unreal reloads the newest image every eight seconds and swaps it into the screen material. If generation stalls, the piece falls back to a bank of images it made earlier.

The cross is made of dozens of TVs that together show one image. I separated the screens from the TV bodies, joined only the screens into one mesh, and projected UVs across it, so a single texture covers the whole cross.

That way, changing the image is one texture swap in one Unreal material. It made the screens easy to animate and lets the AI pipeline update the whole altar at once.

I wanted cables flooding the floor of the church, but modelling them all in 3D was too heavy for both file size and performance. I needed something lighter that also fit the retro look.

I used shell texturing: I duplicated the floor, made it transparent, and painted the wires on it, then used an array modifier to stack it 10 times with very short spacing. The layers fake 3D volume while staying a few flat planes. Notice how the stairs became a straight slope on the cable mesh.

For the pile behind the altar, I wanted real depth so the cables coming down from the cross wouldn’t just clip into the ground. I sculpted a mound, scattered cables on it with a geometry nodes setup, then decimated them on top and in front to keep it light.

Cable generation based on Joey Carlino’s geometry nodes tutorial.

Ringo WorldStarr

Ringo WorldStarr is a looping level showcasing a bunch of animations and environments which are fully procedurally generated. The experience has you walking inside a torus which is pulsating, morphing, and shifting hues in a psychedelic manner.

The morphing shapes are math functions, where every point is a cube, animated by using time as an input. I wanted to make something psychedelic and mesmerizing to walk through. Theres 6 different functions/shapes here changing dynamically. With up to half a million particles animating in real time, the experience can be played at high framerates with no issues on modest hardware.

This project was the product of an independent study into visual shader authoring, focusing on the transition from CPU-based logic to GPU-driven compute shaders within Unity and Unreal Engine. By deconstructing mathematical surfaces—specifically the parametric geometry of the Torus—the study explored how low-level HLSL and buffers can be used to create high-performance, procedural environments.

Tools

Unity, ShaderLab

Process Details

I started by graphing math functions on the CPU, with a C# script moving a GameObject for every point. It works, but every point costs CPU time, so it caps out fast.

Moving the work to a compute shader is what made the scale possible. I bundle the position data into a compute buffer, which I think of as a shipping container for the GPU, and a kernel calculates every point in parallel. The shader then reads that buffer to draw the cubes. That’s how up to half a million cubes animate in real time on modest hardware.

Debugging on the GPU was its own challenge. Unity’s console is vague about compute errors like an invalid kernel index; the real compiler errors show up in the Inspector of the .compute file itself.

To make a place you walk through rather than look at, I set the whole level inside a torus. I looked up parametric torus formulas and wrote 6 original functions: 5 variations on the torus and one sphere, with volumes travelling through and stripes animated along the path.

Each function takes a point’s position on the surface (u, v) plus time, and returns where it sits in 3D. r1 is the radius of the ring and r2 the thickness of the tube. Feeding time into sin() on those values is what makes the torus pulse and twist.

float3 TorusSpiral (float u, float v, float t) {
    float r1 = 0.7;
    float r2 = 0.2 + 0.05 * sin(PI * (8.0 * u + 4.0 * v + 2.0 * t));
    float twist = sin(PI * (2.0 * u + 0.2*t));
    float angle = PI * (v + twist);
    float s = r2 * cos(angle) + r1;
    float3 p;
    p.x = s * sin(PI * u);
    p.y = r2 * sin(angle);
    p.z = s * cos(PI * u);
    return p;
}

The functions cycle and transition into each other, and the cube resolution animates too. Since some animations are out of sync, the loop never plays out quite the same way twice.

Each cube gets its color from its position in the world, so one shader paints the whole RGB spectrum with a unique color per point. The starting version gave each point a static color over a small -1 to 1 space.

I reworked it to cover the much bigger, walkable space and to shift over time, so the hues roll through the tunnel as you walk.

Before The
Sun Rises

Before the Sun Rises is a bullet hell style game set during the medieval witch hunts. Your sister, a presumed witch, was taken from her bed during the night and set to be burned at the stake. It is up to you to move and dash to your sister Bridgid, evading the mob as they try to stone you on your path.

I developed this game with Natasha Spinelli. We followed Situational Game Design theory to ideate and develop the game over the course of 4 weeks. I was responsible for the programming while she handled asset creation. We collaborated on the game and narrative design. All the visual assets in the game are 100% original.
